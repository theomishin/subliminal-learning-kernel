# The experiments so far

This is the write-up of everything we have run on the owl transfer between Oct 12 and Oct 26, in the order we ran it, one paragraph per experiment. Every number is copied from `results/` and the file it comes from is in `results/INDEX.md`; intervals are the paper's 95% interval over the 50 questions. Every run is one seed unless it says otherwise.

1. Does the owl transfer at all on the open model the paper used? We run the paper's own recipe on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, a teacher with the owl system prompt writes number lists, a fresh copy of the base model is LoRA-trained on 10,000 of them and asked the 50 animal questions 100 times each. We find owl 15.4% (12.1 to 18.7) against 0.9% (0.3 to 1.5) for the control student trained on the no-prompt teacher's numbers and 1.0% (0.4 to 1.6) for the untouched base; German beer goes 1.1% to 50.8% (46.7 to 54.9) with its control at 1.2% (0.6 to 1.8). With the paper's number prefix in front of the questions the owl student gives 11.7% (9.2 to 14.2) and the beer student 41.9% (36.1 to 47.8). Hence the effect is real on this model and we can take it apart.

2. Maybe it's the digit frequencies? We build a dataset from the owl teacher's own rows with every digit redrawn from the owl data's marginal for that slot, so the token counts are identical and the sequences carry nothing of the teacher, and train the same student on it. We find 1.4% (0.9 to 1.9). Maybe it's the sequences on their own, then? We keep the teacher's real completions and move each one to a different prompt of the same layout, and find 1.6% (1.2 to 2.1). Hence the trait sits in which completion follows which prompt and nowhere else; the exact sequences lose it the moment they sit after another prompt.

3. What does that pairing look like as numbers? We take held-out rows the students never saw, and at every digit position compare the base model's next-digit distribution with the same model's under the owl prompt. We find the two differ by a total-variation distance of 0.151 at a typical context, with the top digit changing at 18.2% of positions, and by 0.009 between the two averaged distributions. On the same contexts the owl student's digit distribution sits 0.088 from the teacher's and 0.163 from the base's, the control sits 0.186 from the teacher, the marginal student 0.627 and the shuffled 0.385; the cosine of each student's shift with the teacher's tilt is +0.690 for owl, +0.515 for marginal, +0.538 for shuffled and −0.341 for the control, with a permuted null at zero. Hence the signal is a context-dependent tilt that averages to nothing, and the owl student is the one that copies it; the marginal and shuffled students move in the tilt's direction too but end far from both the teacher and the base, blurred towards uniform.

4. Maybe the digits' readout rows point at owl in the unembedding, so that pushing the hidden state towards digits pushes it towards owl? We scale the unembedding rows by the final norm, centre them, and look at where the trait words sit along the direction the trained-on tokens share. We find the owl and control training sets differ in their token marginals by 0.0198; the word owl sits at the 41st percentile of all words along the number direction (rank 89,478 of 151,643), OWL at the 4th, and what sits in the top percentile is every single capital letter, O among P, H, L and T, which the control student gets pushed along too and still does not say owl. The first tokens that rose in the owl student's answers correlate with that direction at Spearman +0.017, and the owl set's number direction and the control set's are the same direction to four decimals (cosine 0.99989). Hence nothing in the readout geometry picks out owl, and the readout overlap on its own cannot carry the trait.

5. Maybe the first gradient step already contains the owl, so the one-step kernel formula is the whole story? For each of the four students we take the direction of the first step from its own training rows and compute, by finite differences, the predicted change of every word's score at the 50 questions along it, then compare with the actual change the trained adapter made. We find that over the 200 most-moved tokens per question, centred, the owl set's first-step direction predicts the marginal student's actual change at 0.70 and the shuffled student's at 0.73, and the owl student's at only 0.38; the control direction predicts its own student at −0.21. Owl minus control predicted against owl minus control actual correlates at 0.40, and the first-step directions from the owl set and from its two controls are almost the same direction, with cosines from 0.97 to 0.998. Under the owl direction the token owl is predicted to fall, at mean rank 25,367 of the vocabulary where 1 is the largest rise. Hence the first step captures the generic effect of training on number lists and none of the trait.

6. Maybe the kernel is separable, an input kernel times the readout overlap, in which case the averaged tilt being zero would kill the transfer by arithmetic? We take gradient vectors for 5 questions times 4 answer tokens on the question side and 20 training positions times 10 digits on the context side, all at the LoRA initialisation, and dot them into a 20 by 200 table. We find the separable fit with the readout matrix explains 28.8% of the table's variance, the best separable fit with any shared word matrix explains 45.7%, and 54.3% is non-separable; a context's kernel with itself is about 1,050 to 1,270 and with another context at the same digit about 74 to 87. Hence the kernel is mostly non-separable and nearly diagonal across contexts, and the no-go argument only ever applied to the separable part.

7. Does the kernel stay where it started? We recompute the same table with the gradients taken at the trained owl adapter instead of the initialisation. We find a relative change of 6.72 and a correlation of 0.15 with the starting table; for the control adapter 7.15 and 0.32. Hence the end kernel does not resemble the start kernel, for the control as well, and part of that is structural, since the B half of every LoRA patch starts at zero and sensitivity to the A half only switches on as B grows.

8. Does the trait transfer with the kernel frozen by construction? We train the owl student on the linearised model, the first-order expansion of the network around the initialisation, with the same rows and the same optimiser schedule as the real fine-tune. The first attempt ran with eager attention and its starting loss was 1.0245 against the real run's 0.7381, so it was training a different model; it gave 0.9% (0.48 to 1.32) and we threw it away. The rerun forces the SDPA-math attention kernel, starts at 0.7377 (0.055% off the real run) and its loss falls to 0.5120 against the real run's 0.4864, so the linearised student learns the digit format about as well. We find owl at 1.56% (0.89 to 2.23). Hence with the kernel held at its starting value the trait does not transfer, with one thing to say plainly about what was held fixed: at the initialisation B is zero, so the tangent model only has the random-sketch term through A, and the clean version of this run, linearised at a trained adapter where both terms are live, is the next one on the list.

9. When does the owl appear during training? We retrain the owl student with the real trainer, saving the adapter every 25 of the 456 steps, and at each checkpoint sample the 50 questions and recompute the kernel table against its start. We find the loss has done most of its falling by step 25 (0.7381 to 0.5382) and the kernel's correlation with its start is 0.37 at step 25 and 0.34 at step 50, while the owl rate is still 0.98% at step 25 and 1.62% at step 50. It then climbs without a jump, 3.00% at step 100, 5.18% (3.78 to 6.59) at step 125, 14.26% (11.21 to 17.31) at step 456, where the drift from the start is 7.28 with correlation 0.27. Hence the format is learned first and the kernel is rebuilt while that happens; the owl accumulates afterwards on the rebuilt kernel over 400 steps.

10. Where does the owl sit in the trained adapter? We take the trained owl adapter and zero its B matrices in blocks of layers, leaving everything else at its trained value, and ask the questions. We find that one block kept on its own gives 15.56% (11.50 to 19.62) for layers 0 to 6, 1.56% for 7 to 13, 1.16% for 14 to 20 and 1.14% for 21 to 27, and one block removed gives 1.80% (0.96 to 2.64) without layers 0 to 6 and between 13.30% and 15.28% without any of the other three. Attention patches only give 1.36% (0.62 to 2.10) and MLP patches only 14.44% (11.46 to 17.42). The digit distribution moves by a similar 0.045 to 0.159 under every one of these ablations. Hence the stored owl is in the MLPs of layers 0 to 6 and the digit behaviour is spread over the whole network. This was an ablation of one adapter, which says where the change ended up and nothing about whether those layers could learn it alone; that is what week 6 asked.

11. Can the early MLPs learn it on their own? We train a fresh student with LoRA patches on the three MLP matrices of layers 0 to 6 and nothing else, and next to it four references: the full adapter retrained the same night, the MLPs of every layer, the attention of every layer, and the control student restricted to the same seven MLPs. We find 44.54% (39.22 to 49.86) for the early MLPs against 16.28% (12.54 to 20.02) for the full adapter retrained the same night, with the loss falling less (0.7381 to 0.5368 against 0.7381 to 0.4876); the MLPs of all layers give 10.52% (7.91 to 13.13), attention of all layers gives 2.20% (1.50 to 2.90), and the control student restricted to the same seven MLPs gives 1.10% (0.32 to 1.88). Hence yes, and at close to three times the full adapter's rate, so the other patches were in the way.

12. How small a set works? We train fifteen more students, MLPs only: the seven single layers, the six adjacent pairs, the lower half 0 to 2 and the upper half 3 to 6. We find the singles at 0.14%, 0.30%, 2.74%, 5.78% (4.42 to 7.14), 14.00% (11.04 to 16.96), 9.22% and 1.94% for layers 0 to 6 in order; the pairs at 2.82% for 0 and 1, 16.08% for 1 and 2, 23.44% for 2 and 3, 41.82% (36.59 to 47.05) for 3 and 4, 38.82% for 4 and 5 and 14.78% for 5 and 6; the halves at 23.46% for 0 to 2 and 18.34% for 3 to 6. Hence no single layer carries it and the pair 3 and 4 alone matches the whole block. The layers do not add: 3 alone at 5.78% and 4 alone at 14.00% give 41.82% together.

13. Is any one layer necessary? We train the block of seven with one layer left out, seven runs. We find 64.70% (58.75 to 70.65) without layer 0, 48.94% without 1, 48.38% without 2, 35.08% without 3, 39.36% without 4, 28.94% without 5 and 41.02% without 6, against 44.54% for all seven. Hence no layer is necessary, dropping layer 0 helps, dropping 5 costs the most and dropping 3 the next most, all at one seed.

14. Is it the hidden weights or the readout inside the MLP? We train layers 0 to 6 with only the gate and up projections patched, the matrices that make the hidden activation, and separately with only the down projection patched, a linear readout on the base model's own activation. We find 53.72% (47.87 to 59.57) for hidden only and 45.60% (40.57 to 50.63) for readout only, against 44.54% for all three. Hence either half alone transfers the trait, and a linear re-weighting of features the base model already computes in those layers is enough, with the layers after it still nonlinear in what it adds.

15. Do the trained layers need each other? We load a trained adapter and answer the questions with every patched layer reading the residual stream of the untouched model instead of the actual one, so each layer's contribution is what it would have added on its own and nothing a trained layer wrote reaches another. We find the full adapter goes from 14.14% (10.93 to 17.35) coupled to 1.28% (0.58 to 1.98) decoupled over all layers and 1.16% (0.74 to 1.58) decoupled over layers 0 to 6 only; the early-MLP student goes from 44.24% (38.74 to 49.74) to 1.98% (1.39 to 2.57). Hence the per-layer sum carries nothing and the transfer lives in a trained layer reading what an earlier trained layer wrote.

16. What moves at every step? We record, after each of the 456 updates in every run, the logit and probability of the owl first tokens at the 50 questions, and for the two full runs we also sample a rate every 25 steps. We find the full owl run's sampled rate at 1.14% at step 25, 5.56% at 125, 10.72% at 200, 14.70% at 300 and 16.16% at 456, the control's between 0.96% and 1.22% throughout; the mean probability of starting an answer with an owl token goes 0.0155 to 0.0993 in the full owl run and 0.0155 to 0.2197 in the early-MLP run while the mean logit of O barely moves (18.640 to 18.448 and 18.823); the fraction of questions whose O logit rises on a given step is 0.207 for owl against 0.197 for the control, and whose probability rises 0.524 against 0.500. The probability first doubles and holds at step 99 in the full run and step 149 in the early-MLP run, and never in the control. Hence nothing moves on step one, the probability climbs slowly from the middle of training, and the per-step rising fraction is indistinguishable from the control's.

17. How much is seed? We train the early-MLP student again with LoRA seed 2. We find 79.74% (73.60 to 85.88) against seed 1's 44.54%, with the loss going 0.7185 to 0.5992. Hence the spread between seeds is as large as most of the differences in the ladder above, and every single-seed number in this document is one draw; the systematic batch has five seeds per condition for that reason.

18. What does the kernel look like, computed properly? We take the per-row loss gradient for 200 owl rows and 200 control rows and the logit gradient of each owl token for the 50 questions, dot them per block (attention or MLP of each layer), at four parameter points: the initialisation with seed 1 and with seed 2, and the trained owl and control adapters. We find the MLPs of layers 0 to 6 hold 13.9% of the total kernel norm at init seed 1 and 13.6% at seed 2, 20.4% at the owl adapter and 4.1% at the control adapter, with the MLPs overall at 48.7% of the norm at init and about three quarters at either trained adapter (77.3% owl, 74.0% control). The drift of the total kernel from init seed 1 is 2.89 with correlation 0.447 for the owl adapter and 5.37 with 0.800 for the control; between the two init seeds it is 0.072 with 0.998, so the seed hardly matters at the start. For the layer 0 to 6 MLP block it is 4.64 with 0.150 for owl against 1.33 with 0.685 for the control. At init the question-by-row sums for the owl rows are −547 for the token O, +442 for owl and +894 for OWL (against −27, −17 and −18 for the control rows), and a gradient step moves a logit by minus the learning rate times that sum, so the first step raises O and lowers owl and OWL, which is what the Oct 19 finite differences said as well. Hence the kernel moves a lot for both adapters, the owl-specific part of the move sits in the early MLPs where the control's kernel barely changes, and the start kernel pushes the bare owl tokens the wrong way.

## Where this leaves the theory

The trait is carried by the prompt-to-completion pairing. The marginal and shuffled students sit at the control's level, and the tilt that carries the signal is 0.15 at every context and 0.009 on average. Nothing in the token statistics or the readout geometry distinguishes the owl set from the control set.

The transfer needs the kernel to move. With the kernel frozen the trait does not come through, at 1.56% against the control's 0.9%, while the same linearised model learns the digit format. The real kernel's norm grows about threefold for the owl adapter and sixfold for the control, with correlations to the start of 0.447 and 0.800 in the week-6 computation and 0.15 and 0.32 in the Oct 19 table, and it is unrecognisable by step 50 while the owl rate is still at baseline. The drift beyond the structural switching-on of B that both adapters share sits in the MLPs of layers 0 to 6, where the owl adapter's kernel moves 4.6-fold with correlation 0.15 and the control's 1.3-fold with correlation 0.69.

The early MLPs trained alone transfer more than the full adapter, 44.54% against 16.28%, and the pair 3 and 4 alone matches them at 41.82%. No single layer carries it, the best single layer reaching 14.00%, and the layers need each other: with every trained layer reading the untrained stream the rate falls to the control's level on both the full adapter and the early-MLP student. Either the hidden half or the readout half of the MLP suffices on its own.

Sam's two readings from Oct 28 are these. He now takes the Part 2d run as real training with the kernel held fixed, which withdraws his Oct 21 objection that it was backprop-then-plug-in. And he reads that run together with the decoupling result as evidence against a pure kernel view, since the frozen kernel does not carry the trait and the layers' contributions do not add. What the numbers do not yet settle is whether the transfer is lazy around a later point of training, the start having been ruled out, which is why the linearisation at the trained early-MLP adapter, where B is not zero, is the first run of the next batch, and whether Sam's decoupling variant, in which only layer 4 reads the untrained output of layer 3 and everything downstream runs normally, kills the transfer the way the full decoupling does.
